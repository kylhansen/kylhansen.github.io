Introduction

What do you expect this blog post to be about? Seriously, take 10 seconds (no more) and make a guess.

It has been demonstrated1 that one is most effectively primed to learn new knowledge and information when one tries to make predictions about the new information to be received. This allows the brain to more readily find fitting connections for the new information to be slotted into the network of one’s pre-existing knowledge, solidifying old pathways while generating new ones. Later on, that connection (and the information itself) is solidified through the practice of retrieving the knowledge. This makes learning naturally into a three-act play.2

The Purpose of the Lab Book

You might be familiar with this three-act structure in the form of lab reports, where you have a hypothesis, the experiment itself, and the results/discussion. Teaching is, in many ways, a continual experiement where none of the variables are held constant. Because this three-act structure is so widely used both as a way of building one’s knowledge, and for building on one’s knowledge through experimentation, I plan to implement a similar outline for evaluation of my classroom experiments as a teacher.

This blog, then, can be considered my personal lab book. In it, I intend to express my expectations, my decisions, and my reactions/reflections throughout my teaching career. Because my own new learning will be interwoven with these stages, other notes and reflections on various readings/ideas will likely be strewn about as well.3

This Post as a Preface

If this blog is a lab book, this post is the introduction to my first attempt at keeping a lab book. In it, I intend to set the stage for how I expect to maintain this lab book throughout the upcoming term, and (perhaps) beyond. This is a meta-post, in the sense that it is itself a preview of my experimental method; later, I should end up making some meta-notes about the experiment of this experimental method, and after that, review some of the things that wend well and what went poorly, and how to revise my method.4

The Experiment

This upcoming term, I plan to implement three phases to the experiment of my teaching. I will call these three stages the PRE-views, the INTER-views, and the RE-views of the term.5

20-ish hours later

I’m now returning to this blog post. I’m trying to implement active approaches to stopping early, before diminishing returns set in6 on work projects. This might mean leaving some ideas unfinished, and returning to them at a later time, working in brief regular sessions to lower the barrier to re-entry.7 I failed at this yesterday, but I’m back.

I expect this blog itself to provide me opportunities for breif, regular sessions8 of preparation and reflection as I process my teaching, research, and service as a professor. In the rest of this section, I outline some of my current working philosophy behind the three-act structure (PRE-, INTER-, and RE-views) I expect my notes to take.

PREviews

Influenced by past reviews, aimed at future interviews, I plan to implement previews as a method of laying out plans and predictions for upcoming terms, units, lectures, projects, etc. Ideally, every “large-scale” item (e.g., each semester and/or new course preps, research projects, service endeavor) will begin with me making plans and predictions about that project. This is where I will break down the larger task into what I predict will be smaller-scale items, and why I made the decisions that I did. These smaller items may also possibly merit their own subjection to the pre-, inter-, re-view structure, but often they will be independently processed through “interviews”.

INTERviews

Influenced by past previews, aimed at future reviews, I plan to conduct interviews as a way of touching base on the core elements of a larger project. This is where I will record how I approached specific events, the events that actually transpired, and my (near-immediate) reflections on the outcomes. I call these “inter”views not only to keep with the “__view” theme, but also to remind myself that this is a time to ask and answer questions about the events that transpired, and how they relate to the project at large. Sometimes, these “inter”views may seem more like public journal entries, and I’m kind of okay with that. I will not use identifying student information, and will not stories involving students without their express permission. Eventually, my responses will be compiled at a larger scale on a given project in “reviews”.

REviews

Influenced by past interviews, aimed at informing future previews, I plan to conduct reviews as a systematic way of processing both my predictions and reactions to events, and summarizing some of my strengths, weaknesses, continued questions, new ideas, etc. in order to grow in the future. I hope these reviews eventually become useful tools for my own compilation into P/R/T documents, and provide a little peek into how I process the events of my classroom.
